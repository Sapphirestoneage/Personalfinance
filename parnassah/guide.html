Guide

The words this site uses, the checklists worth walking before the big decisions, and the handful of rules that change the arithmetic for a family with many children in day school.

The rules that change the arithmetic

    What to ask the tuition committee

    1. What does the school count as income, and what does it deduct? Retirement contributions, a second child in nursery, support for a parent: each school reads the return differently.
    2. Is the reduction set once for all the children, or child by child? Most set a family figure.
    3. What else is on the bill: the building fund, the dinner obligation, busing, the technology fee, the trip in eighth grade?
    4. Is there a sibling discount, and from which child?
    5. When is the bill due, and what does the payment plan cost? Ten months from August is common; a lump sum sometimes earns a discount.
    6. Does the school accept a 529 withdrawal directly, and will it write the receipt the plan needs?

    Before you sign for the house

    1. Walk it on a Friday night. The distance to the shul is the whole point; the eruv boundary is the second.
    2. Add tuition to the mortgage before you decide, not after. The home page here does that.
    3. Ask the town what the tax will be after the sale, not what the seller paid. A reassessment can add a third.
    4. Keep the emergency fund after closing. A house that empties it has cost more than its price.

    The halachic paperwork

    Insurance for a large family

    • Term life, both parents. Enough years of income to carry the tuition ahead, not the usual ten. The parent who earns less still needs cover: replacing what they do costs money too.
    • Disability. Tuition is due whether or not you can work. Group cover through work is the cheap start; an own-occupation policy is the full answer for a professional.
    • Umbrella liability. Cheap, and worth it for a family with teenagers driving and Shabbat guests on the stairs.

    The community's own tools

    • Gemachs. Free-loan societies, and lending closets for everything from wedding gowns to folding tables. A gemach loan is interest-free by design; treat it as a debt all the same, and pay it on time so it is there for the next family.
    • Tomchei Shabbos and the local funds. Where a share of tzedakah does the most good closest to home.
    • The shul's own help. Dues relief is quiet and common; ask the executive director, not the board.

    The words

    Parnassah
    Livelihood: what the household earns and lives on.
    Maaser kesafim
    A tenth of income given to tzedakah. Chomesh is a fifth.
    Tuition assistance
    The reduction a school's committee sets for a family, usually as one share applied to every child.
    The year in Israel
    A year of yeshiva or seminary after high school, priced as a fifth year of tuition on the tuition page.
    FLOP
    Flowers, liquor, orchestra, photography: the wedding costs a chatan's family conventionally carries.
    Heter iska
    The document that makes a loan between Jews with interest permissible as a partnership.
    Shtar chatzi zachar
    A document, from the era of the Rishonim, that lets a daughter inherit alongside her brothers under Torah law; today usually a clause in a halachic will.
    Empty is not zero
    A blank box means not entered. Every figure that needs it says so instead of guessing a number.
    Assumed
    A figure taken from this site's dated reference bands because the family has not typed its own. It is marked wherever it appears.
    The lean month
    What a month costs with nothing optional in it: the floor the emergency fund is measured against.

    • Reference figures carry a date and a confidence; the footer of every page says where they come from.
    • Nothing here is halachic, tax or legal advice. The site does the arithmetic so the conversation with your rav, your preparer and your lawyer starts from a number.
