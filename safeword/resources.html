Guide 1

When a platform closes your account: the 48-hour list

For a subscriber site, a clip store, a cam site, a payment app or a bank. In order. Do the first four before you post about it anywhere.

  1. Screenshot everything you can still see. The balance, the payout history, the message they sent, the terms page as it reads today. A held balance is easier to claim with a picture of it.
  2. Do not open a new account on the same site under another name. It is the fastest way to lose the held balance for good and to be banned from the appeal.
  3. Move nothing in a panic. If the closure is at a bank or an app, the other accounts you hold there may be reviewed next. Take your money out through the normal route, not in one large transfer at midnight.
  4. Write the appeal the same day, in plain words, once. What you were told, what the balance is, the payout you are owed, the date. No argument about the policy; a request for the money and the reason.
  5. Switch the streams that landed there. On the planner's Streams page, point each one at its backup rail. If there is no backup, that is the first thing to set up this week, not the appeal.
  6. Tell your regulars where to find you, by the channel you own. An email list, a link page, a phone number. The platform's message system is not yours anymore.
  7. Open the fund page and read the number. The safeword fund exists for this day. Months it carries you is the only figure that matters this week; everything else can wait for the appeal.
  8. Take the tax jar off the table. The jar is not an emergency fund. If the month is short, the fund pays for it. The jar is April's.
  9. In a week, write down what you learned about the rail. Which keyword, which policy, which review. Then set the closure risk on the Rails page to what you now know.
Guide 2

The tax jar in one page

The only tax number you can act on is a share. Not a bill in April, not a bracket, not a rate on a chart: of every hundred dollars that lands from the work, how many go in the jar the same day. Once you have that number the rest is a habit.

  1. The jar is a share of what lands, not of what you charged. The site's cut and the card fee never reach you, so they are not in the jar. Start from what actually arrives.
  2. Self-employment tax is on almost all of it. Roughly fifteen percent on about ninety-two percent of your profit, before income tax starts. It is the part that surprises people who only ever had a job.
  3. The costs of the work come off first. The space, the listings, the screening, the phone, the accountant. Keep them in one place all year; the profit the return sees is what lands minus these.
  4. A vanilla job withholds its own. The jar is for the work. The job's tax is already taken out; the planner counts it apart so you do not double up.
  5. The safe harbour is the rule most calculators skip. Pay what you owed last year, in four parts, and there is no penalty whatever this year brings. It turns a guess into a known number.
  6. Fill it the day money lands. A separate account, a transfer the same day, the share from the planner. If the share is 24, a $500 session puts $120 across before you spend the rest.
  7. Empty it four times a year. April 15, June 15, September 15, January 15. What is left over after the return is yours.

The planner's Taxes page computes the share from your streams, your costs and your state, and shows the working line by line. It is an estimate for planning, not advice about your return.

Guide 4

The eight papers that put your people on the list

If you are hurt, held or gone, the law reaches for a list: spouse, then parents, then siblings. A partner of ten years, a submissive who lives with you, a polycule, a leather family: not on it. Eight papers change that. In the order that matters most.

  1. Beneficiary forms on every account. Retirement accounts, life insurance, anything with a transfer-on-death form. These pass by the form, not by the will, and a blank form means the estate, which means the list. Log in to each one tonight.
  2. A will. Who gets what the forms do not cover, and who handles it. Signed and witnessed the way your state requires. Many states have simple statutory forms; a lawyer who has seen a chosen family before is worth the fee.
  3. Medical power of attorney. Who speaks for you in a hospital, and who is let in the room. Most states have a free form. Give the person a copy.
  4. Financial power of attorney. Who can pay your rent and reach your accounts if you cannot. Show the bank once so it is not a fight later.
  5. The digital file. Where the content accounts, the wallet keys, the two-factor codes and the passwords are, and who may use them. A platform balance dies with the login.
  6. The name file. Which accounts, leases and bills are under which name, working or legal, so the person handling things can find them all.
  7. The emergency plan. If you are hurt, held or arrested: who to call first, who has a key, who feeds the animals, where the lawyer or bail money is.
  8. A letter of wishes. Not a legal paper. What you want said and not said, who should be told what, what happens to the content.

Guide 5

What to ask a bank before the practice's money goes there

Most closures are not personal. A rule, a keyword, a review team, a deposit pattern. You cannot make a bank safe, but you can find out how it fails before it fails on you.

  1. "What kinds of business are outside your terms?" Ask it plainly. Adult entertainment is on many lists. A bank that names it will close you; one that does not name it may still.
  2. "What triggers a review?" Large cash deposits, many small transfers from apps, a name on a transfer that a filter dislikes. You are asking so you can keep your pattern boring.
  3. "If you close the account, how do I get the balance?" A cheque to the address on file in thirty days is a common answer. Plan for thirty days without it.
  4. "Can I hold a second account here in a different name?" Usually not, and it is the wrong question anyway. The second account goes at a second bank.
  5. "Do you offer a business account without a registered business?" A sole proprietor account under your legal name keeps the practice's money apart from your own without a company to explain.

The rule that survives every answer: two banks, a backup way to take a card, a lean month in cash or somewhere nobody can freeze, the practice's money apart from your own, and nothing large left sitting on a platform. The planner's Rails page checks all five.

Guide 6

The first $1,000 you keep

Not the first thousand you earn. The first thousand that is still there a month later. The order matters more than the amount.

  1. Know the floor. Every low month at once, after every cut. Write it down. Everything else is measured against it.
  2. Start the jar before the fund. The tax share off every dollar, from the first dollar. A fund built out of untaxed money is a fund with a lien on it.
  3. One lean month somewhere nobody can freeze. Cash, or a place with no terms of service. This is the first thousand's job.
  4. The second bank, before the second thousand. Open it while nothing is wrong. Banks do not open accounts for people whose last bank just closed one.
  5. Then the fund proper. A share of every month, the same day, into the account you have marked as the fund. The planner says how many months until it is full and how many nos it buys.
  6. Then the long game. When the fund is full, the share it was taking goes to the retirement account, not back into the month.
