What the couples' finance books skip

  • There are three of you. Or four. Equal shares punish the one who earns least; shares in proportion to income are the usual fair answer, and the planner does the arithmetic so nobody has to argue it.
  • Somebody is quietly under water. A split that looks fair on paper can leave one person below their own lean month. The floor check says so before it becomes resentment.
  • Money moves inside the dynamic. An allowance, a tribute, a purse one person holds. It is play, and it is also money; the protocol page checks it the way you would check any scene.
  • Financial submission needs a floor. A drain is a scene. Rent is not. The giver keeps their lean month and their fund set-aside whatever the scene says, and the arrangement has a cap and a word that ends it.
  • The law has a list and you are not on it. Spouse, parents, siblings. Without a will, beneficiary forms and a medical power of attorney, a hospital can keep your partner out of the room and the state can hand your things to a family you left.
  • The name file. Which lease, which bill, which account is under which name. Whoever handles things needs to find them all.

What the House Session looks like

1. Everyone's numbers, once

What each person brings in and what each person needs to stay housed and fed. First names or scene names.

2. The split

Equal, in proportion, or agreed shares. Each person's amount and what is left above their floor, on the screen, where everyone can see it.

3. The protocol

If money moves inside the dynamic: written down with the five guardrails, and a review date on the calendar.

4. The papers

The eight, weighted by what each decides. Who does which one first, and which state forms are free.
