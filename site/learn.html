How FIRE works, on one page.

Financial independence is one multiplication and one habit. The multiplication sizes the pot. The habit, a savings rate held for years, fills it. Everything else on this site is detail around those two.

1 · The multiplication

Twenty-five times what you spend

A pot of invested money can pay out a small slice of itself every year, more or less forever, because the rest keeps growing. The slice most people plan around is 4% a year. Turn that round and the pot has to be 25 times a year of spending. Spend $40,000 a year and the number is a million. Spend $30,000 and it is $750,000.

Three things follow, and they are the whole game.

  • Spending sets the number, not income. A raise you spend moves the target up. A raise you keep moves you toward it twice: more saved, and a smaller number to reach.
  • The 4% is a choice, not a law. A cautious plan draws 3.5% and needs 29 times spending; a bold one draws 4.5% and needs 22. Your number shows all four side by side.
  • Everything is in today's dollars. The app uses a return above inflation, 5% by default, so the number you see is the number you would recognise.

In the app: The Number keeps this multiplication, in six sizes, with your own figures in it. Its assumptions are on the page and you can move them.

2 · The habit

The savings rate is the whole timeline

How long it takes does not depend on how much you earn. It depends on what share of your take-home pay you keep. Someone who keeps half their pay reaches 25 times their spending in about the same number of years whether they earn $40,000 or $400,000. The table is that arithmetic, run from nothing, at the app's default return above inflation.

3 · The order

Where the next dollar should go

Not every dollar has the same job. A dollar that clears a 24% credit card beats a dollar in the market; a dollar that captures an employer match beats both. The ladder below, the Financial Order of Operations popularised by The Money Guy Show, puts the jobs in order. You stand on the first step you have not finished.

4 · The sizes

Six versions of one number

The community has names for the number at different sizes of life. They are not six calculations. They are the same multiplication with a different month of spending in it, plus two twists: Coast discounts the full number back to today, and Barista lets part-time pay cover part of the bill.

5 · The words the app uses

Five families, three boxes, seven bands

DAITE
Debt, assets, income, taxes, expenses. Every number a household holds belongs to one of the five, and the rooms are arranged by them. Type a figure in the room that owns it and every other room reads it.
FAT
Food, accommodation, transportation. The three boxes whose total is the lean month: what you would spend if everything optional stopped. Lean FIRE is 25 times this.
DRAFTT
Seven shares of your income measured against seven bands: the scorecard's measuring stick. Each ratio is judged on its own axis; nothing sums them into a score you could game.
The Ledger
Every number the app can hold, as one row each: some you know, some you look up, some are computed. Facts are entered there, once, and read everywhere.
Triple D
Drawdown, duration, depth. The shaded band on the long chart: how far the money could fall, for how long, and how deep, on the way down.

Every term in the app, in one plain sentence each.

6 · The back half

You cannot size the mountain until you know how you come down it

The number assumes a way down: a withdrawal rate, an order to draw accounts in, a floor of income that does not depend on the market. A plan that stops at "reach the number" is half a plan. The other half is the sequence of bad years you might meet first, the age the money has to last to, the bridge between stopping work and the accounts you cannot touch yet, and what is left behind.
